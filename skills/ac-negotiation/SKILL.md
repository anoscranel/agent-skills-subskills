# Negotiation

## Overview

Negotiation is the craft of reaching agreement with a counterparty whose interests partly oppose yours, where no single side can simply impose the outcome. Its central insight is that power in a negotiation comes less from arguing well at the table than from the alternative you hold if no deal is reached. Preparation dominates performance: the party who knows their own best alternative, their reservation point, and the other side's likely interests usually out-negotiates the more fluent talker.

This skill covers the deal-making conversation itself, not the relationship around it: ac-team-collaboration owns the internal team, ac-ma-transaction-execution owns the mechanics of closing a corporate acquisition, ac-persuasive-writing owns marketing copy, and ac-behavioral-investing owns the investor's own biases. The stance throughout is that a good agreement is one you would willingly sign again, not one that leaves the other side defeated.

Primary sources: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Getting_to_Yes> and <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Best_alternative_to_a_negotiated_agreement>.

## When to use

- Preparing for a negotiation and setting your reservation point and BATNA.
- Deciding whether to claim value or create value across issues.
- Recognising and countering negotiation tactics without escalating.
- Handling a hard bargainer, an impasse, or a power imbalance.
- Reviewing a negotiation afterward to improve the next one.

## When NOT to use

- For day-to-day teamwork and internal team norms (use ac-team-collaboration).
- For M&A deal structuring and closing mechanics (use ac-ma-transaction-execution).
- For persuasive marketing or sales copy (use ac-persuasive-writing).
- For an investor's own decision biases (use ac-behavioral-investing).

## Status and honest limits

Negotiation skill improves the odds and the quality of an agreement but never guarantees one: some deals should not be made, and walking away is often the correct result. The craft assumes both parties can choose to say no; where one party cannot genuinely refuse, the interaction is coercion, not negotiation, and the techniques here do not apply. Tactics knowledge is for defence, not manipulation — using pressure tactics on a weaker counterparty produces agreements that unravel and relationships that end. Nothing here is legal or financial advice on a specific deal.
