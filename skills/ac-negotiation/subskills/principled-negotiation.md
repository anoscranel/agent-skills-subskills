# Principled negotiation

## Standard

Principled negotiation, set out in Getting to Yes by Fisher, Ury and Patton, is a method for reaching agreement without either positional stubbornness or concession for its own sake. It rests on four principles: separate the people from the problem, focus on interests rather than positions, invent options for mutual gain, and insist on objective criteria. The method aims at a wise agreement reached efficiently and without damaging the relationship.

The fourth principle is the one most often dropped under pressure. Objective criteria are standards independent of either party's will: market value, precedent, professional norms, legal requirements, cost of replacement, a neutral third party's assessment. When a disagreement is settled by reference to such a standard rather than by who concedes first, neither party has to lose face, and the result is defensible afterward. This is also the answer to a hard position: rather than arguing against the position, ask what objective basis supports it, and hold the discussion to standards both sides can accept.

Primary sources: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Getting_to_Yes> and <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principled_negotiation>.

## Decision rules

- Separate the people from the problem; attack the problem, not the person holding it.
- Negotiate over interests, not positions; ask why a position is held before countering it.
- Generate several options before judging them; do not decide on the first acceptable one.
- Insist on objective criteria independent of either party's will to settle disagreements.
- Ask for the objective basis of a hard position instead of arguing against it.
- Aim for an agreement you can defend later, not merely one the other side signs now.

## Procedure

1. Name the people issues and the substantive issues separately at the outset.
2. Elicit the interests behind each stated position, yours and theirs.
3. Brainstorm options for mutual gain without committing to any yet.
4. Agree on the objective standards that will judge the options.
5. Evaluate options against those standards and package the agreement.
6. Test the final agreement: would you sign it again tomorrow, and defend it to others?

## Anti-patterns

- Conceding to preserve the relationship at the cost of your interests.
- Arguing positions back and forth and calling it negotiation.
- Deciding on the first workable option before generating alternatives.
- Settling by who is more stubborn rather than by objective standards.
- Letting the people problem and the substantive problem contaminate each other.

## Done checklist

- [ ] People separated from the problem.
- [ ] Interests elicited behind each position.
- [ ] Multiple options generated before judging.
- [ ] Objective criteria agreed and applied.
- [ ] Objective basis requested for any hard position.
- [ ] Final agreement tested for later defensibility.
