# Difficult negotiations

## Standard

The hardest negotiations are those where the counterparty will not cooperate, the emotions run high, the power is one-sided, or the parties are stuck. Each calls for a different response, and the common error is to meet all of them with more pressure, which usually entrenches the other side.

A hard bargainer who attacks positions and people is answered by refusing to play the same game: go to the balcony, as the method puts it, meaning step back from the exchange long enough to think, then return to interests and objective standards rather than trading attacks. Strong emotions, yours or theirs, are data to be acknowledged, not problems to be suppressed; naming the emotion and giving it a hearing often releases the logjam that arguing facts cannot. A power imbalance changes the strategy rather than the goal: the weaker party's best protection is a strong alternative and objective standards, not appeals to fairness that the stronger party can ignore. An impasse is broken by changing something other than the price, such as the issues, the timing, the parties, or a neutral third party, rather than by conceding to end the discomfort.

Primary sources: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Getting_to_Yes> and <https://www.pon.harvard.edu/>.

## Decision rules

- Step back before responding to an attack; do not trade positions or insults.
- Acknowledge emotions explicitly instead of arguing past them.
- Against a stronger party, rely on your alternative and objective standards, not on appeals.
- Break an impasse by changing the issues, timing, parties, or forum, not by conceding.
- Use a neutral third party when direct bargaining has stalled.
- Recognise when no agreement is better than a bad one and be willing to end it.

## Procedure

1. Pause before reacting to an attack or a hard position.
2. Name and acknowledge the emotions in the room, yours included.
3. Return the discussion to interests and to objective standards.
4. Reassess your alternative and the counterparty's need for the deal.
5. If stuck, change an issue, the timing, the participants, or bring in a neutral.
6. End the negotiation if the available outcome is worse than your alternative.

## Anti-patterns

- Meeting a hard bargainer with equal hardness.
- Suppressing or ignoring emotions and expecting facts to settle them.
- Appealing to fairness when the other side holds all the power.
- Conceding to escape an impasse.
- Staying in a negotiation whose best outcome is worse than walking away.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Pause taken before responding to attacks.
- [ ] Emotions acknowledged explicitly.
- [ ] Discussion returned to interests and standards.
- [ ] Alternative reassessed under the power imbalance.
- [ ] Impasse addressed by changing a variable other than price.
- [ ] Walk-away exercised when the outcome is worse than the alternative.
