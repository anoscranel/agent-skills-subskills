# Patient information and health literacy

## Standard

Patient information tells a patient what they need to understand and do: a patient information leaflet, a discharge summary, a consent form, a condition or medication guide. The reader is not a clinician, and the document must work for someone with no medical background and possibly limited health literacy. Health literacy — the ability to obtain, understand and use health information — varies widely, and the document is written for the reader who struggles most, not the confident reader.

Plain language is the method. Short sentences, everyday words, the active voice and a named actor replace medical jargon; where a technical term is unavoidable, it is defined in plain words at first use. The most important information comes first, and instructions are concrete and actionable: what to do, when, and what to watch for. Readability is checked, and the document is tested with real patients where possible.

Comprehension is verified, not assumed. The teach-back approach asks the reader to explain the information back in their own words, revealing what the document failed to communicate. A patient document that a reader cannot act on has failed, however accurate it is.

Primary sources: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_literacy> and <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plain_language>.

## Decision rules

- Write for the reader with the lowest health literacy, not the confident reader.
- Plain language throughout; define any unavoidable technical term at first use.
- Most important information first; instructions concrete and actionable.
- Check readability; test with real patients where possible.
- Verify comprehension with teach-back, never assume it.

## Procedure

1. Identify what the patient must understand and do.
2. Draft in plain language, most important information first.
3. Replace or define every technical term.
4. Make instructions concrete: action, timing, warning signs.
5. Check readability and test with patients if available.
6. Verify with teach-back and revise what readers cannot explain back.

## Anti-patterns

- Medical jargon in a patient document.
- Important information buried in the middle.
- Vague instructions with no concrete action.
- Assuming comprehension without checking it.
- A document a patient cannot act on.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Written for low health literacy.
- [ ] Plain language; technical terms defined.
- [ ] Most important information first.
- [ ] Instructions concrete and actionable.
- [ ] Comprehension verified, not assumed.

Numbers need special care for patients. Absolute frequencies in plain terms — 'about 1 in 100 people' — are easier to act on than percentages or relative risks, and a small diagram or icon array can make a risk concrete where words alone fail. What the patient should do next is always explicit: who to contact, how soon, and which warning signs mean seeking help urgently rather than waiting for a routine appointment.
