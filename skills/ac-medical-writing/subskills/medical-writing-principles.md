# Medical writing principles

## Standard

Medical writing communicates medical and clinical information accurately for a specific audience: a clinician, a regulator, a patient, or a payer. The craft rests on three obligations: accuracy to the source data, fitness for the audience's knowledge and needs, and honesty about what the evidence shows. The audiences differ sharply — a regulatory reviewer expects precise data and standard structure; a patient needs plain language and actionable information; a clinician expects the evidence and its limits.

The register follows the audience. Clinician and regulatory writing is precise, technical and evidence-dense; patient writing is plain, concrete and free of jargon. The evidence base is the same; the presentation changes. The writer's accuracy obligation does not: every statement must trace to the source data or a cited source, and the strength of a claim must match the strength of the evidence behind it.

The boundary is absolute: medical writing is communication craft, never medical advice. The writer does not diagnose, recommend a treatment, or tell a reader what to do clinically. Those belong to clinicians. The writer's role is to make accurate medical information clear and usable for its intended audience.

Primary sources: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_writing> and <https://www.icmje.org/>.

## Decision rules

- Identify the audience first: clinician, regulator, patient or payer; the register follows.
- Every statement traces to source data or a cited source; nothing is asserted from memory.
- Claim strength matches evidence strength; never overstate a result.
- Precision for clinician and regulator audiences; plain language for patients.
- Medical writing is never medical advice; clinical decisions belong to clinicians.

## Procedure

1. Identify the audience and the document's purpose.
2. Assemble the source data and references the document must rest on.
3. Set the register and terminology for that audience.
4. Draft with every claim traceable to a source.
5. Check each claim's strength against its evidence.
6. State the not-medical-advice boundary where the document could be read as advice.

## Anti-patterns

- A claim stronger than the data behind it.
- Jargon in a patient-facing document.
- An unsourced clinical assertion.
- Writing that reads as a treatment recommendation.
- One register used for every audience.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Audience identified and register matched.
- [ ] Every claim traced to a source.
- [ ] Claim strength matches evidence.
- [ ] Register suits the audience.
- [ ] No treatment recommendation or clinical advice.
