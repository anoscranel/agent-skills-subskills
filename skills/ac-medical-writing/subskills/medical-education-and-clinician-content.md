# Medical education and clinician content

## Standard

Medical education content teaches clinicians and health professionals: continuing medical education modules, clinical review articles, treatment summaries, conference coverage and case-based learning. The reader is a professional who expects the evidence and its limits, so the content is evidence-dense, technically precise and honest about uncertainty — a different register from patient information and from public science news.

The content is built on the current evidence. A clinical review or education module synthesises the relevant studies and guidelines, states the strength of the evidence behind each claim, and distinguishes established practice from emerging or contested findings. Where the evidence is weak or disputed, that is stated, not smoothed over. The reader must be able to judge how much weight to give each point.

Educational design supports learning. Objectives are stated, the content is organised around them, and case-based or problem-based formats are used where they aid application. The content is accurate, balanced and free of promotional influence — education is not marketing, and a sponsor's product is not favoured in what is presented as independent education.

Primary sources: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_writing> and <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_literacy>.

## Decision rules

- Content is evidence-dense and technically precise for a professional reader.
- Strength of evidence stated behind each claim; established versus emerging distinguished.
- Weak or disputed evidence stated, not smoothed over.
- Educational objectives stated and content organised around them.
- Content is balanced and non-promotional; education is not marketing.

## Procedure

1. Define the educational objectives and target professional audience.
2. Assemble the relevant studies and guidelines.
3. Organise content around the objectives, with case or problem formats where useful.
4. State the strength of evidence behind each claim.
5. Check balance and remove promotional influence.
6. Verify clinical accuracy through qualified review.

## Anti-patterns

- A claim presented without its evidence strength.
- Emerging findings presented as established practice.
- Disputed evidence presented as settled.
- Educational content that favours a sponsor's product.
- Content with no stated educational objective.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Educational objectives and audience defined.
- [ ] Content built on current evidence.
- [ ] Evidence strength stated per claim.
- [ ] Balanced, non-promotional content.
- [ ] Qualified clinical review completed.

References are the reader's route to the evidence. Each substantive claim carries a citation the clinician can follow to the underlying study or guideline, and the citation is current — practice moves, and a module built on superseded guidance teaches outdated care. Where guidelines disagree, the disagreement is presented with the reasons each body gives, so the reader understands the basis of the difference rather than being handed a single unexplained recommendation.
