# Evidence and data reporting

## Standard

Reporting medical evidence means presenting what a study actually found, at the strength the data supports. The core obligations: report results completely, present effect sizes honestly, and never let a statistic overstate the finding. A medical writer who mishandles a number can mislead clinicians and patients about a treatment's real benefit or harm.

Absolute and relative measures are never conflated. A treatment that cuts an event rate from 2% to 1% is a 50% relative reduction but a 1 percentage-point absolute reduction; quoting only the relative figure overstates the benefit. Report absolute risks and, where meaningful, the number needed to treat, alongside any relative measure. The same honesty applies to harms: adverse events are reported with their rates and severity, not minimised.

The study's limits travel with its results. A single study suggests rather than proves; an association is not a cause; a surrogate endpoint is not a clinical outcome; a result in one population may not generalise to another. Reporting the finding without its limitations is a form of overstatement.

Primary sources: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinical_study_report> and <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Publication_bias>.

## Decision rules

- Report results completely, including unfavourable and null findings.
- Absolute risks and absolute effect sizes reported alongside any relative measure.
- Adverse events reported with rates and severity, never minimised.
- The study's limitations stated with its results.
- Surrogate endpoints distinguished from clinical outcomes.

## Procedure

1. Extract the study's actual results, including null and negative findings.
2. Compute and report absolute risks and effect sizes with any relative figures.
3. Report adverse events with rates and severity.
4. State the study's design limits and population.
5. Check each number against the source analysis.
6. Read the result as a clinician would: is the real benefit or harm clear?

## Anti-patterns

- Quoting only a relative risk reduction.
- Omitting null or unfavourable results.
- Minimising adverse events.
- A surrogate endpoint presented as a clinical outcome.
- Results reported without the study's limitations.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Complete results reported, including null and negative.
- [ ] Absolute measures with relative measures.
- [ ] Adverse events with rates and severity.
- [ ] Limitations stated with results.
- [ ] Numbers verified against source analysis.

Confidence intervals belong beside the point estimate. An effect reported without its interval invites the reader to treat an imprecise result as exact, and a wide interval around a promising number is often the most honest thing on the page. Where a result is not statistically significant, that is reported as an absence of demonstrated effect, not as proof of no effect, and the interval shows what effects the data can and cannot rule out.
