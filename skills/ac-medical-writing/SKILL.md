# Medical writing

## Overview

Medical writing is the craft of communicating medical and clinical information accurately for a specific professional or patient audience: clinical study reports and regulatory submissions, patient information, medical education content, and the documents that carry trial evidence. It is distinct from science communication to the public and from scholarly writing — the audiences, regulatory expectations and accuracy obligations are different, and an error can affect care.

This skill owns the craft. Its neighbours: ac-science-communication explains research to the public; ac-academic-writing covers scholarly papers; ac-technical-writing covers product and process documentation; ac-plain-language covers general plain-language rewriting. Medical writing sits at the clinical end: accuracy to the source data, the right register for clinician, regulator or patient, and publication ethics.

The hard boundary: this is writing craft, never medical advice. Treatment and clinical decisions belong to clinicians. The writer's job is accurate, clear, ethical communication of medical information.

Primary sources: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_writing> and <https://www.icmje.org/>.

## When to use

- Writing a clinical study report, protocol summary or regulatory document.
- Writing patient information or medical education content.
- Reporting trial results or medical evidence.
- Working through authorship, disclosure or publication-ethics questions.

## When NOT to use

- For explaining science to the general public (use ac-science-communication).
- For scholarly papers and theses (use ac-academic-writing).
- For product and process documentation (use ac-technical-writing).
- For general plain-language rewriting (use ac-plain-language).

## Status and honest limits

Medical writing is the craft of accurate medical communication; it never substitutes for clinical judgement and never recommends a treatment. The not-medical-advice boundary is load-bearing and stated throughout. Regulatory and publication requirements vary by jurisdiction, region and document type, and change over time — the leaf states the craft principles and names the relevant standard bodies, not a current regulatory rule for a specific submission. Nothing here certifies clinical accuracy for a particular document; every document requires qualified clinical and regulatory review.
