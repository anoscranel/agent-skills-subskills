# Change management

## Overview

Most change efforts fail not on the solution but on adoption: the system ships, the process is documented, and people keep working the old way. Change management is the discipline that treats adoption as the deliverable. It is distinct from project management, which produces the change, and from team collaboration, which is day-to-day teamwork. The change manager's question is not "is it built?" but "are people actually working differently, and will they still be next quarter?"

This skill owns the people side of change: the case for change, readiness and impact, the change model and roadmap, the sponsor coalition and communication, training and adoption support, and the reinforcement that prevents reversion. Its neighbours: ac-project-management runs the project that produces the change; ac-team-collaboration is ongoing teamwork; ac-platform-engineering drives adoption of an internal platform specifically; ac-negotiation is one-to-one deal-making. Change management is the organisational-adoption layer across all of them.

Primary sources: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Change_management> and <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ADKAR>.

## When to use

- When an organisation must adopt a new system, process, structure, or way of working.
- When a delivered project is not being used as intended.
- When planning how to communicate, train, and sustain a change.
- When resistance to a change needs to be understood and addressed.

## When NOT to use

- For running the project that produces the change (use ac-project-management).
- For day-to-day team dynamics (use ac-team-collaboration).
- For internal platform adoption specifically (use ac-platform-engineering).
- For one-to-one deal negotiation (use ac-negotiation).

## Status and honest limits

Change management cannot guarantee adoption: it raises the odds by treating the people side as planned work, not by promising an outcome. A communication plan is not a change plan, and a training session is not adoption. The honest measure is observed behaviour change that persists, not messages sent or people trained. Change models are scaffolding, not formulas — the same model that worked in one organisation can fail in another whose culture, power structure, and history differ. Where the change is genuinely harmful to a group, change management does not make it acceptable; that is a decision for the organisation's leadership, not a communications problem.
