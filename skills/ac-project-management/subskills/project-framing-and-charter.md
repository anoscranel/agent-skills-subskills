# Project framing and charter

## Standard

The charter is the project's founding document: it names the project, its purpose, its objectives, the high-level scope, the sponsor who authorises it, and the authority the project manager holds. It exists to align everyone on what the project is before anyone plans how to run it. A project that starts without a charter starts without agreement on what it is for.

Objectives are written to be measurable. "Improve the system" is not an objective; "reduce checkout latency below two seconds at the ninety-fifth percentile by end of Q3" is. Success criteria are stated up front, in the same measurable terms, so that closure can be judged against them rather than against whoever argues loudest at the end. The charter also records what is explicitly out of scope, because the boundaries are where projects most often fail.

Primary sources: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_charter> and <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_management>.

## Decision rules

- Write the charter before detailed planning; it authorises the work and names the sponsor.
- State objectives in measurable terms with a target and a timeframe.
- Define success criteria up front so closure is judged against them.
- Record what is explicitly out of scope, not only what is in.
- Name the sponsor and the project manager's authority level.
- Identify the high-level constraints and assumptions the plan will rest on.

## Procedure

1. Write the purpose: the problem or opportunity the project addresses.
2. State objectives as measurable targets with timeframes.
3. Define success criteria and how they will be measured at closure.
4. Record high-level scope and the explicit exclusions.
5. Name the sponsor, the project manager, and the authority granted.
6. List the top constraints, assumptions, and high-level risks.

## Anti-patterns

- Starting detailed planning before the charter is agreed.
- Objectives with no measurable target or timeframe.
- Success criteria invented at closure to fit whatever was delivered.
- Scope stated only as what is included, never what is excluded.
- A charter with no named sponsor or authority.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Charter written and agreed before planning.
- [ ] Objectives measurable with timeframes.
- [ ] Success criteria defined up front.
- [ ] Exclusions recorded explicitly.
- [ ] Sponsor and authority named.
- [ ] Constraints and assumptions listed.

The charter also serves as the reference when priorities collide later. When a stakeholder demands a change that would alter the objectives, the question is answered against the charter rather than against whoever is most insistent in the room. A short, agreed charter prevents a long argument about what the project was supposed to be, and it gives the project manager a documented basis for saying no.
