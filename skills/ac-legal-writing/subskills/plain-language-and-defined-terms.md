# Plain language and defined terms

## Standard

Plain legal language makes a legal document readable by the people it binds, without losing the precision that makes it enforceable. The two aims are in tension only when the drafter confuses habit with necessity. Most obscurity is habit: "prior to" for "before", "in the event that" for "if", "utilise" for "use". Replacing these loses nothing. But a defined term of art carries a meaning the law has settled, and replacing it with a friendlier word can change the obligation.

Defined terms are the document's controlled vocabulary. Each is defined once, capitalised, and used with total consistency — never a near-synonym for variety. A good defined term is short, meaningful, and used only if it appears more than once; defining a term used once adds indirection for no gain. The definitions section is read as a checklist against the body: every capitalised term in the body is defined, and every definition is used.

Primary sources: <https://www.plainlanguage.gov/> and <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plain_language>.

## Decision rules

- Replace habitual legalese with plain equivalents; preserve genuine terms of art.
- Define each term once, capitalise it, and use it with total consistency.
- Define only terms used more than once; one-off terms stay inline.
- Prefer short, meaningful defined terms over initials.
- Reconcile the definitions section against the body in both directions.
- Test readability on a reader who is not a lawyer.

## Procedure

1. List every capitalised term in the body.
2. Confirm each is defined once and used consistently.
3. Remove or inline terms used only once.
4. Replace habitual legalese with plain equivalents.
5. Shorten sentences and name the actor in each obligation.
6. Read the result with a non-lawyer and fix what confuses them.

## Anti-patterns

- "Heretofore", "whereas", "aforementioned" kept for tone.
- The same concept named two ways in one document.
- Defined terms that are never used, or used but never defined.
- Initialisms that force the reader to hold ten abbreviations at once.
- Simplifying a term of art into a word that changes the legal effect.
- Sentences that run past the point a reader can hold.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Habitual legalese replaced; terms of art preserved.
- [ ] Each defined term defined once and used consistently.
- [ ] No unused definitions and no undefined capitalised terms.
- [ ] Obligations in active voice with named actors.
- [ ] Sentences within a readable length.
- [ ] A non-lawyer can follow the document.
