# Persuasive legal writing

## Standard

A brief persuades a decision-maker within the rules of evidence and candour. It is not a louder memo: it selects and orders the strongest facts and authority for the client's position while remaining scrupulously accurate about the record and the law. Misstating a fact or hiding binding adverse authority destroys the credibility the argument depends on, and courts remember.

The structure is deliberate. The fact statement tells the story as a narrative in which the facts themselves argue — concrete, chronological, and favourable without distortion. Point headings state the conclusion of each argument as a full sentence, so the table of contents reads as the argument. Each argument leads with the strongest point, applies the governing rule to the favourable facts, and meets the strongest counter-argument directly rather than ignoring it. The relief sought is stated precisely.

Primary sources: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brief_(law)> and <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_writing>.

## Decision rules

- State facts accurately; never stretch the record or omit material adverse facts.
- Write point headings as full-sentence conclusions of each argument.
- Lead each argument with the strongest point and the strongest authority.
- Address the strongest counter-argument directly and answer it.
- Make the fact statement a narrative that argues by selection and order, not distortion.
- State the precise relief sought at the end.

## Procedure

1. Identify the decision-maker, the standard of review, and the relief sought.
2. Draft the fact statement as a concrete chronological narrative.
3. Frame each issue as a point heading that states its conclusion.
4. Build each argument: rule, application to favourable facts, counter-argument, answer.
5. Order arguments strongest first.
6. Close with the precise relief and check the record citations.

## Anti-patterns

- Point headings that name a topic instead of stating a conclusion.
- Ignoring binding adverse authority the court will find anyway.
- Adjectives and rhetoric doing the work the facts should do.
- A fact statement that reads as argument rather than narrative.
- Weakest argument first, burying the strong one.
- Relief left vague or broader than the record supports.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Standard of review and relief identified.
- [ ] Fact statement is accurate, concrete, and chronological.
- [ ] Point headings are full-sentence conclusions.
- [ ] Each argument applies rule to facts and answers the counter-argument.
- [ ] Strongest argument leads.
- [ ] Relief stated precisely; record citations checked.
