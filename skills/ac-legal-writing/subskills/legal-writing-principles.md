# Legal writing principles

## Standard

Legal writing is judged by whether the reader can apply it without guessing. The reader is a judge deciding, a client acting, an opposing party performing, or a regulator checking — and each needs a different register. Precision outranks elegance: a sentence that reads beautifully but leaves an obligation ambiguous has failed. The goal is exactness expressed as plainly as the subject permits, not the ornament that signals "legal".

The first discipline is audience and purpose. A brief persuades; a memo informs; a contract binds; a letter instructs. Each form has a register and a structure the reader expects. The second discipline is that plain language and precision are usually allies, not enemies: most legalese is habit, not necessity. But some terms of art — "consideration", "negligence", "fee simple" — carry a settled legal meaning that a plain paraphrase would destroy, and the craft is knowing which words must survive.

Primary sources: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_writing> and <https://www.plainlanguage.gov/>.

## Decision rules

- Identify the reader and the document's purpose before choosing a register.
- Prefer the plain word unless a term of art carries meaning the plain word loses.
- One idea per sentence; an obligation that needs a second reading is rewritten.
- State obligations in active voice with a named actor: "the Tenant shall pay", not "payment shall be made".
- Treat every ambiguous modal ("may", "shall", "must", "should") as a deliberate choice.
- Never sacrifice a precise legal meaning for readability.

## Procedure

1. Name the reader and what they must do after reading.
2. Choose the document form and its expected structure.
3. Draft in active voice with named actors and concrete verbs.
4. Mark each modal and confirm it says what is intended.
5. Replace legalese with plain equivalents except genuine terms of art.
6. Read aloud; any sentence that stumbles is rewritten.

## Anti-patterns

- Legalese kept for its own sake: "heretofore", "whereas", "party of the first part".
- Passive voice that hides who must act.
- "Shall" used for every verb, blurring command, future, and obligation.
- Long nested sentences with qualifications piled on qualifications.
- Simplifying a term of art into a word that changes the legal meaning.
- Writing for other lawyers when the reader is a client.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Reader and purpose named.
- [ ] Register matches the document form.
- [ ] Active voice with named actors throughout.
- [ ] Modals chosen deliberately.
- [ ] Terms of art preserved, habit legalese removed.
- [ ] Every sentence parses on first reading.
