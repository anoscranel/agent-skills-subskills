# Legal analysis and memos

## Standard

The objective legal memo predicts how a legal question is likely to be resolved; it does not argue for a preferred answer. Its discipline is honesty about both sides: the writer states the issue, the governing rule, how the rule applies to the facts, and the conclusion — including the facts and authority that cut against the conclusion. A memo that hides the weak points is advocacy dressed as analysis and misleads the reader who must decide.

The working structure is IRAC: Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion. State the issue as a question that the facts answer, not an abstraction. State the rule with its source and its elements. Apply the rule element by element to the specific facts, showing the reasoning rather than asserting the result. Conclude with the most likely outcome and its confidence, naming the strongest counter-argument. Variants (CRAC, CREAC) put the conclusion first for a reader who wants the answer up front; the analysis is the same.

Primary sources: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IRAC> and <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_writing>.

## Decision rules

- State the issue as a specific question the facts resolve.
- State the governing rule with its source and elements before applying it.
- Apply each element to the facts with reasoning, never bare assertion.
- Present the strongest counter-argument and the facts that support it.
- Conclude with the likely outcome and an honest confidence level.
- Keep the memo objective; persuasion belongs in the brief.

## Procedure

1. Frame the issue as a question tied to the material facts.
2. Identify and state the governing rule with its source.
3. Break the rule into its elements.
4. Apply each element to the facts, showing the reasoning.
5. Address the strongest contrary authority and facts.
6. State the conclusion, its confidence, and the open questions.

## Anti-patterns

- A conclusion stated without the reasoning that reaches it.
- Only the authority that supports the preferred answer.
- Abstract issue statements the facts cannot answer.
- Rule statements with no source.
- Advocacy presented as objective analysis.
- False certainty where the outcome is genuinely contested.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Issue framed as a specific question.
- [ ] Governing rule stated with source and elements.
- [ ] Each element applied to the facts with reasoning.
- [ ] Counter-argument and contrary facts presented.
- [ ] Conclusion with confidence level stated.
- [ ] Memo reads as analysis, not advocacy.
