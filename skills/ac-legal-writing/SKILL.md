# Legal writing

## Overview

Legal writing is a precision craft: the reader is a judge, a client, an opposing party, or a regulator, and a word can change an obligation. Good legal writing is not ornate — it is exact, structured, and as plain as the subject allows. This skill teaches the writing craft for legal documents: how to analyse an issue with IRAC, structure a contract, write a persuasive brief, use defined terms, cite authority, and review the result. It does not teach the law and never gives legal advice; a qualified lawyer owns the substance of any real matter.

Primary sources: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_writing> and <https://www.plainlanguage.gov/>.

## When to use

- When drafting or revising a legal document: contract, memo, brief, letter, policy.
- When a legal text must be made plainer without losing precision.
- When structuring legal analysis with IRAC or citing legal authority.
- When reviewing a draft for consistency, readability, and correct citation.

## When NOT to use

- For legal advice or interpreting the law for a real situation — consult a qualified lawyer.
- For technical documentation (use ac-technical-writing).
- For business emails and memos (use ac-business-writing).
- For general plain-language rewriting (use ac-plain-language).

## Status and honest limits

This skill teaches writing craft, never law. It does not interpret statutes, assess a legal position, or substitute for a qualified lawyer who owns the substance of any real matter. Citation conventions and drafting norms vary by jurisdiction and court; the record must state which applies rather than assuming one. Plain language is a goal with limits: some terms of art carry precise legal meaning that plain paraphrase would destroy, and the craft is knowing which words must not be simplified. Nothing here is legal advice.
