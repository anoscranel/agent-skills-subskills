# Psychological safety and trust

## Standard

Psychological safety is the shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking — that a member can ask a question, admit a mistake, or disagree without being punished or humiliated for it. Amy Edmondson's research established it as a driver of team learning and of effective work teams. Its mechanism is information flow: in an unsafe team, problems stay hidden because raising them costs the raiser, so the team learns about its failures late and from someone else.

Safety is built by behaviour, not declaration. A leader who responds to bad news with curiosity instead of blame teaches the team that truth is welcome; one who shoots the messenger teaches silence. It is not the same as comfort or low standards — the safest teams hold high bars and disagree openly, because the risk people can take includes honest disagreement. And it is not niceness: a "nice" team that avoids hard truths is often the least safe of all.

Primary sources: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological_safety> and <https://doi.org/10.2307/2666999>.

## Decision rules

- Respond to bad news, questions, and mistakes with curiosity before judgment.
- Leaders model fallibility: admit their own errors and uncertainty openly.
- Invite dissent explicitly and thank the person who disagrees, in public.
- Treat silence in meetings as a signal to investigate, not as agreement.
- Hold high standards AND high safety; never trade one for the other.
- Measure by whether bad news travels fast, not by whether people seem happy.

## Procedure

1. Assess the current climate: do people raise problems, or hide them?
2. Model fallibility from the top; name your own mistakes and what you learned.
3. Respond to each raised concern by exploring it before evaluating it.
4. Invite dissent by name in decisions; thank and protect the dissenter.
5. Address any humiliation or blame response immediately and visibly.
6. Track whether problems surface earlier over time.

## Anti-patterns

- Declaring "this is a safe space" while punishing the first honest comment.
- Confusing safety with avoiding disagreement or hard feedback.
- A leader who never admits being wrong.
- Reading a quiet meeting as a healthy one.
- Shooting the messenger, then wondering why problems arrive late.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Bad news met with curiosity, not blame.
- [ ] Leaders model fallibility openly.
- [ ] Dissent invited and thanked publicly.
- [ ] Silence investigated, not assumed to be agreement.
- [ ] High standards kept alongside safety.
- [ ] Measured by speed of bad news, not apparent happiness.
