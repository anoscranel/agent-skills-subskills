# Framing the decision

## Standard

Most bad decisions are good answers to the wrong question. Framing is the discipline of stating, before any analysis, exactly what is being decided, who decides it, and within what constraints. The frame names the choice in one sentence with a verb and an object: not "the market" or "our strategy" but "whether to launch product X in market Y by quarter Z". A frame that cannot be stated as a choice is not yet a decision; it is a topic.

The frame also names the decider and the decision's boundaries. Who owns this choice and could actually commit to it? What is fixed (budget, deadline, law, physics) versus what is itself a choice that could be opened? And what is the decision's relationship to other decisions: is it standalone, the first of a sequence, or one branch of a larger choice? A frame drawn too narrowly optimises a piece and loses the whole; drawn too broadly it becomes unanalysable. The test is that the frame captures every consequence the decider would actually weigh, and no more.

Primary sources: <https://doi.org/10.1017/cbo9780511611308.004> and <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decision_analysis>.

## Decision rules

- State the decision as one sentence with a verb, an object, and a timeframe.
- Name the decider who can commit, not the team that discusses.
- Separate fixed constraints from choices that could be reopened.
- Classify the decision: standalone, sequential, or one branch of a larger choice.
- Draw the boundary to include every consequence the decider would weigh.
- Refuse to analyse until the frame is a choice, not a topic.

## Procedure

1. Write the candidate decision in one sentence.
2. Ask "what would a yes and a no each commit us to?" and sharpen the sentence.
3. Name the decider and confirm they can commit.
4. List constraints; mark each as fixed or reopenable.
5. Place the decision relative to related decisions.
6. Test the frame: does it capture what the decider would actually weigh?

## Anti-patterns

- Solving the convenient decision rather than the real one.
- A frame so broad it is unanalysable, or so narrow it misses the point.
- Treating a chosen constraint as fixed when it could be reopened.
- Analysing before anyone can name who commits.
- Confusing a goal ("grow revenue") with a decision ("whether to acquire").

## Done checklist

- [ ] Decision stated as one choice sentence with timeframe.
- [ ] Decider named and able to commit.
- [ ] Constraints split fixed versus reopenable.
- [ ] Decision classified standalone/sequential/branch.
- [ ] Boundary tested against what the decider would weigh.
- [ ] Frame is a choice, not a topic.
