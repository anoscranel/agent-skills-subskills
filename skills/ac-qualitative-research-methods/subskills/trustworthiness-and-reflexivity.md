# Trustworthiness and reflexivity

## Standard

Qualitative rigour is argued through Lincoln and Guba's four criteria, which parallel but do not replicate quantitative validity. Credibility: is the account believable from the data? Transferability: can a reader judge its applicability to a comparable context, supported by thick description? Dependability: is there an audit trail of the decisions taken? Confirmability: do the findings rest on the data rather than on the researcher's preferences? The techniques are specific — prolonged engagement, persistent observation, triangulation of sources and methods, member checking, negative-case analysis, an audit trail, and thick description.

Reflexivity is the methodological acknowledgment that the researcher is the instrument. The researcher's position, assumptions, relationships to participants, and reactions shape what is noticed and how it is read, and these are examined and reported rather than denied. A reflexive journal keeps that examination live. Trustworthiness is not a claim of objectivity; it is a demonstrated, inspectable account of how the findings were produced and how the researcher's influence was handled.

Primary sources: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qualitative_research> and <https://doi.org/10.1093/intqhc/mzm042>.

## Decision rules

- Argue rigour through credibility, transferability, dependability, confirmability.
- Use the specific techniques: triangulation, member checking, negative-case analysis, audit trail.
- Provide thick description so readers can judge transferability.
- Keep a reflexive journal on the researcher's position and influence.
- Rest findings on the data, not on the researcher's preferences.
- Make the production of the account inspectable.

## Procedure

1. Plan credibility techniques appropriate to the design.
2. Triangulate sources and methods; run negative-case analysis.
3. Member-check interpretations with participants where feasible.
4. Keep a reflexive journal and an audit trail throughout.
5. Write thick description of context and participants.
6. Report how each trustworthiness criterion was addressed.

## Anti-patterns

- Claiming objectivity instead of demonstrating trustworthiness.
- Thick description omitted, so transferability cannot be judged.
- Reflexivity reduced to a single biographical sentence.
- Member checking skipped or its results ignored.
- Findings that reflect the researcher's prior commitments.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Four trustworthiness criteria addressed.
- [ ] Triangulation and negative-case analysis used.
- [ ] Member checking done or its absence justified.
- [ ] Reflexive journal and audit trail kept.
- [ ] Thick description provided.
- [ ] Researcher influence reported.

One caution applies to member checking in particular: participants validate recognisability, not the analyst's theoretical interpretation, and a participant's disagreement is data to be examined rather than a veto to be obeyed or an error to be dismissed. The reflexive account records where the researcher's reading diverged from the participant's own, and why the analysis nonetheless proceeded as it did.
