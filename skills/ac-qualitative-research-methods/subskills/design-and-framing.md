# Design and framing

## Standard

A qualitative study begins with a question about meaning, experience, process, or context — something a measurement cannot capture directly. "How do nurses experience handover on a night shift?" is qualitative; "does the new handover reduce errors?" is not. The question is open enough to let the phenomenon show itself and bounded enough to be answerable: a named population, a setting, and a phenomenon. A question that already presumes the answer, or that is really a request for a number, belongs to another method.

The design family follows the question. Grounded theory builds a theory of a process from data systematically collected and analysed; ethnography describes a culture or group through sustained immersion; a case study examines a bounded instance in depth; phenomenology describes the structure of a lived experience. The paradigm — interpretivist, constructivist, critical, pragmatic — is stated, because it determines what counts as knowledge and therefore what the analysis may claim. A design named without its paradigm is a label, not a design.

Primary sources: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qualitative_research> and <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grounded_theory>.

## Decision rules

- Confirm the question is exploratory and meaning-focused, not a disguised hypothesis test.
- Choose the design family by the phenomenon, not by familiarity.
- State the paradigm; it governs what the analysis may claim.
- Name the population, setting, and phenomenon in the question.
- Reject a question that presumes its answer or asks for a number.
- Record the rationale for the design choice against the alternatives.

## Procedure

1. Write the question as an open, bounded exploration of meaning or process.
2. Test it: is the answer a number or a yes/no? If so, reframe or change method.
3. Match the phenomenon to a design family with its stated warrant.
4. State the paradigm and what counts as knowledge within it.
5. Record the design rationale and the alternatives rejected.
6. Carry the design statement into every later methodological decision.

## Anti-patterns

- A hypothesis test dressed as an exploratory question.
- A design named with no paradigm and no warrant.
- Choosing grounded theory because it sounds rigorous while doing descriptive coding.
- An unbounded question that cannot be answered in the available data.
- A question whose answer is already presumed.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Question is exploratory, bounded, and meaning-focused.
- [ ] Confirmed it is not a disguised hypothesis test.
- [ ] Design family matched to the phenomenon.
- [ ] Paradigm stated.
- [ ] Population, setting, phenomenon named.
- [ ] Design rationale recorded with alternatives rejected.
