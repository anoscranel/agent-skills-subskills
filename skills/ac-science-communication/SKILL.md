# Science communication

## Overview

Science communication explains research to people who did not do it: the public, journalists, policymakers, funders, students. The craft is translation, not dilution — the finding must survive intact while the jargon, framing and level of detail change. Its failure modes are distinctive and well documented: hype that overstates a result, false certainty that hides preliminary status, analogies that mislead, and a deficit-model assumption that handing over facts is enough. Good science communication is accurate, accessible, and honest about what is not yet known.

This skill owns the science-to-public craft. Its neighbours: ac-academic-writing for scholarly papers to experts, ac-plain-language for plain-language public documents, ac-narrative-writing for story and talk craft, ac-technical-writing for product documentation, ac-news-writing for general news journalism. The science-communication skill sits at the intersection of research and the public, where the source is a study and the reader is not a specialist.

Primary sources: <https://www.nap.edu/catalog/23674/communicating-science-effectively-a-research-agenda> and <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_communication>.

## When to use

- When explaining a study, finding or scientific concept to a non-expert audience.
- When writing a press release, lay summary, explainer or popular article.
- When communicating uncertainty, preliminary results or contested findings.
- When preparing to talk to journalists or the public about research.

## When NOT to use

- For a scholarly paper aimed at specialists (use ac-academic-writing).
- For plain-language rewriting of standards or public documents (use ac-plain-language).
- For talks, scripts and story structure (use ac-narrative-writing).
- For product and process documentation (use ac-technical-writing).

## Status and honest limits

Science communication cannot guarantee understanding or agreement — audiences bring prior beliefs, and facts alone rarely change minds, which is why the dialogue and framing matter as much as the content. Simplification always loses some nuance; the craft is choosing which nuance to lose without crossing into inaccuracy. The skill never promises that a well-communicated finding will be believed, shared or acted on, and it never treats media pickup as a measure of scientific importance. Where a finding is genuinely uncertain or contested, the honest move is to say so, not to resolve it for the reader.
