# Conveying uncertainty

## Standard

Most research is uncertain, and the honest communication of that uncertainty is a core skill, not a weakness. Preliminary findings, small samples, wide confidence intervals, contested results and outright unknowns must be conveyed as what they are. Presenting a preliminary result as settled is a distinct failure from hype: it misleads by false certainty even when the claim's direction is right.

The craft is to state what is known, what is uncertain and what is unknown, in plain terms. A confidence interval is "the range the true value plausibly lies in", not a single number. "Early results", "one small study", "not yet replicated", "researchers disagree" give the reader an accurate picture of the evidence's weight. Uncertainty is not the same as ignorance — a finding can be well-supported at a range and still be useful. The goal is a reader who understands both the finding and how firmly it is established.

Primary sources: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_communication> and <https://www.nap.edu/catalog/23674/communicating-science-effectively-a-research-agenda>.

## Decision rules

- State what is known, what is uncertain and what is unknown.
- Preliminary and unreplicated findings are labelled as such.
- Ranges and intervals replace false single-number precision.
- Contested findings present the disagreement, not a false consensus.
- Uncertainty is framed as evidence weight, not as "we know nothing".
- Never resolve genuine scientific disagreement for the reader.

## Procedure

1. Separate the finding into established, uncertain and unknown parts.
2. Label preliminary and unreplicated results.
3. Express ranges and confidence in plain language.
4. Where contested, describe the main positions fairly.
5. State what would settle the uncertainty.
6. Check the reader can gauge how firmly the finding holds.

## Anti-patterns

- A preliminary result reported as settled fact.
- A point estimate with no sense of range.
- False balance that implies equal evidence on both sides.
- False consensus that hides real disagreement.
- "More research is needed" used to avoid any conclusion.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Known, uncertain and unknown separated.
- [ ] Preliminary status labelled.
- [ ] Ranges expressed in plain language.
- [ ] Disagreement presented fairly.
- [ ] What would settle it stated.
- [ ] Reader can judge evidence weight.

Numbers help or hinder depending on presentation. Absolute risks are clearer than relative ones: a doubling of a one-in-a-million risk is still tiny, and saying only "twice the risk" alarms without informing. Natural frequencies — "about 2 in every 1,000" — are usually grasped more easily than percentages. Visuals of ranges, such as an interval bar rather than a single point, make uncertainty concrete instead of abstract. The aim is a reader who can weigh the finding, not one who is merely reassured or alarmed by the framing of a number.
