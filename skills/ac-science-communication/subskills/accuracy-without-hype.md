# Accuracy without hype

## Standard

Accessibility and accuracy are not in tension until the writer makes them so. The hype trap is the pressure to make a finding sound bigger than it is: "breakthrough", "revolutionary", "proves", "cures", "could change everything". These inflate a result past what the evidence supports and erode trust when the inevitable correction or non-replication follows. The accurate version is almost always still interesting — a real effect, honestly stated, does not need inflating.

The discipline is to match the claim to the evidence. A single study suggests; it does not prove. A cell-culture or mouse result is not a human outcome. A correlation is not a cause. "May", "suggests", "is associated with" are not weakness; they are accuracy. Where the finding is genuinely large, the evidence itself makes the case and superlatives are unnecessary. The test is whether a careful reader of the paper would recognise the public claim as fair.

Primary sources: <https://senseaboutscience.org/> and <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_journalism>.

## Decision rules

- Match the claim's strength to the evidence's strength.
- One study suggests; it does not prove.
- Animal or in-vitro results are never presented as human outcomes.
- Association is not stated as causation.
- Remove superlatives; let the evidence carry the interest.
- A careful reader of the paper must recognise the claim as fair.

## Procedure

1. Write the finding at the strength the evidence supports.
2. Replace "proves/cures/breakthrough" with the accurate verb.
3. Check model-organism and association claims are labelled.
4. Read the claim against the paper's actual result.
5. Keep the genuine significance; remove the inflation.
6. Have a subject expert confirm the claim is fair.

## Anti-patterns

- "Breakthrough" for an incremental result.
- "Scientists prove" for a single study.
- Mouse-study results written as human benefits.
- Correlation reported as cause.
- Hedged wording stripped out to sound stronger.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Claim strength matches evidence strength.
- [ ] No proof/cure/breakthrough language without warrant.
- [ ] Model organisms and associations labelled.
- [ ] Claim checked against the paper.
- [ ] Superlatives removed, significance kept.
- [ ] Expert confirmed the claim is fair.

The pressure to hype often comes from outside the writer: an institution's communications office, a funder wanting impact, a journalist wanting a stronger angle. The defence is a written claim-to-evidence check before release, so the negotiation happens against the paper rather than against taste. Where a headline overstates the finding, the correction belongs to the writer who supplied the overstatement, and the accurate rewording is offered rather than a vague objection. Accuracy is a position that must be defended with the evidence in hand, not merely preferred.
